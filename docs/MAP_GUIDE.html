What the interactive map shows and how to read it.

What the map shows

The interactive map (index_SBASvsnoSBAS_standalone.html) shows ground velocity across five areas of northern Luzon: how fast the land surface is moving toward or away from a Sentinel-1 satellite orbiting about 700 km overhead, in millimetres per year.

The method is Interferometric Synthetic Aperture Radar (InSAR). It detects movement too small to see or feel, such as a district settling by a few millimetres a year as groundwater is pumped out.

Sustained negative velocity (red) can mean land subsidence from groundwater extraction or movement of unstable slope material. Blue areas may indicate post-seismic rebound or uplift from pressurised groundwater.

Transparent pixels are water bodies or areas where the radar signal was too noisy for a reliable measurement (dense forest canopy, steep terrain shadow).

Two layer types: what makes them different

SBAS Time-Series
  • 17 satellite passes, Aug 2024 – Feb 2025
  • 16 image pairs stacked and averaged
  • Atmospheric noise largely cancelled out
  • DEM error and quadratic ramp corrected
  • A six-month average velocity
Single-Pair Reference
  • Two satellite passes, 12 days apart
  • Aug 15 and Aug 27, 2024 only
  • Heavily contaminated by atmospheric delay
  • No corrections applied
  • Shown for comparison only

The difference is atmospheric noise. Water vapour differs between the two dates of a pair, and that difference creates a spurious signal that can mimic or mask ground movement. A single pair can't separate ground movement from atmospheric delay. Ground movement keeps the same direction and rate from date to date and the atmosphere doesn't, so stacking 16 pairs over six months lets the ground movement come through.

Toggle the two groups on and off in the map panel to see this. Areas with large colour differences between the layers are dominated by atmospheric noise in the single-pair image.

How to use the map

SBAS Time-Series
Toggle the six-month averaged velocity layers on or off. Start here.
Single-Pair Reference
Toggle the raw single-pair layers. Use these to see how much noise the time-series processing removed.
Opacity slider
Adjust transparency of all overlays to see the OpenStreetMap basemap underneath, and compare velocity patterns with roads, rivers and settlements.
Individual checkboxes
Each of the ten overlays can be toggled on its own. Use this to compare one area in both versions (SBAS on, single-pair off, then the reverse).

The basemap needs an internet connection. Opened straight from disk, the basemap tiles show "403 Access blocked"; serve the folder over HTTP (for example python -m http.server) and they load. The velocity overlay images are embedded in the file and display even when offline.

How this was produced

The pipeline:
1. The Sentinel-1 radar satellite (ESA, free public access) passes over northern Luzon every 12 days.
2. NASA/ASF's HyP3 cloud service downloads the raw data, runs interferometry and delivers processed image pairs. It's free within a monthly allowance of 8,000 credits; this stack cost 16 credits per burst, one per image pair.
3. MintPy stacks the 16 image pairs with the SBAS method (Small Baseline Subset), corrects DEM error and a quadratic ramp, and fits a velocity trend to each pixel.
4. The velocity map is rendered as colour PNGs and placed on the Leaflet map.
Sentinel-1 data: Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2024–2025.
Processing: ASF HyP3 (NASA/JPL) + MintPy 1.6.2 (University of Miami).
